# Narrative and visual choices

Start from what the viewer should understand afterwards. Write the causal chain in plain language and identify which links need evidence or illustration. Treat the narration as a spoken explanation, not an essay read aloud.

Choose a structure that serves the material:

- **Mechanism:** establish the starting state, show the input, follow the transformation, and compare the resulting state.
- **Evidence:** show a question, inspect attributable observations, evaluate competing explanations, and state the supported conclusion with its limits.
- **Worked example:** introduce a small concrete problem, perform each step, check the result, and explain where the method stops applying.
- **Misconception:** state a plausible intuition, demonstrate the counterexample, and build the corrected explanation without ridiculing the audience.

For still scenes, make a visual change at each meaningful step: highlight the active part of a diagram, add the next item in a queue, compare two labeled measurements, or reveal the evidence supporting a statement. Keep shared elements stable so viewers can track what changed. Do not animate merely to avoid a freeze detector.

Use screenshots or photographs as evidence only when they actually support the claim. Preserve captions, units, source dates, transformations, and reuse permissions. When procedural or invented imagery illustrates a concept, label it appropriately. Source code licensing does not grant rights to third-party imagery, voices, fonts, brands, or data.

Restyling accepted content should preserve narration bytes, meaning, and accepted timing. A different story structure may require a revised script and recording; identify that dependency before recreating speech. Keep information density, text size, narration speed, and pauses appropriate for the audience. Review the final encoded version at its intended viewing size.
